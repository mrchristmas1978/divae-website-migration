Choosing a CMS: How to Find the Right Platform

Define your requirements, evaluate CMS platforms and avoid common selection pitfalls

Anne Brosch

Content management systems (CMS) have come a long way in recent years. What was once mainly used to manage individual websites is now an important part of a company’s overall digital experience.

​

Content is no longer limited to websites. It needs to work across landing pages, apps, portals and commerce platforms. At the same time, companies are facing growing demands around personalization, international rollouts, governance and speed to market. That means choosing a CMS is about more than comparing features. The real question is which platform fits your business, supports your processes in the long run and works well with the systems you already have in place.

​

In this article, we look at what really matters when choosing a CMS, which criteria to consider and which common mistakes to avoid during the selection process.

What Is a CMS – and What Options Are There?

A content management system (CMS) provides the foundation for creating, managing and publishing digital content. It allows editorial, marketing and other business teams to manage content themselves instead of relying on IT for every change. Content can then be published across different channels and touchpoints.

​

Today, a CMS is therefore a central part of a company’s digital experience. Modern platforms come in different shapes and architectures, with the main differences being flexibility, integration and complexity.

​

Two approaches are particularly common:

  • Integrated, DXP-Oriented Platforms
  • Composable / API-First Platforms

​

Which approach makes sense for your organization depends on your existing systems, internal processes and long-term digital goals. Some companies need a centralized platform with strong governance and standardized processes. Others value flexibility and a more decoupled setup that allows them to respond quickly to new requirements.

So the decision is not just a technical one. It also has a strategic impact: it shapes how you manage, deliver and develop content in the future – and how well your CMS fits into your wider digital setup

When Is It Time for a New CMS?

Most companies don’t replace their CMS because it suddenly stops working. More often, the problems build up over time.

​

Here are some of the signs we commonly see with outdated CMS platforms:

  • Campaigns and landing pages take too long to publish
  • Marketing and editorial teams still depend on IT for simple changes
  • Content and assets have to be maintained in several places
  • International websites become increasingly difficult to manage consistently
  • Personalization and omnichannel delivery are hard to scale

​

There can also be technical reasons to rethink the existing setup:

  • Performance, security and compliance requirements are increasing
  • The current CMS is outdated and difficult to maintain or develop further
  • Replatforming or moving to the cloud is difficult with the existing architecture
  • Integrations with MarTech, commerce and data systems are limited
  • There is no central approach to content and asset management
  • Teams lack a consistent content operating model

​

If several of these points sound familiar, it may be time to take a closer look at your current CMS. Limited integration options, scalability issues and growing demands on the digital experience are often good reasons to reconsider the existing setup.

Learnings From the Field: Start With the Target State, Not the Platform

A CMS doesn’t operate in isolation. The modernization project of an international company, supported by our experts at diva-e Conclusion, is a good example of why the bigger picture matters. When selecting the new platform, the company looked closely at how it would work with its DAM, analytics and personalization tools, as well as its e-commerce system and existing IT landscape.

​

One major advantage was that the company had already defined a clear target state for its future digital experience, including roles and governance. Business, editorial, IT, security and operations teams worked together to define concrete use cases and look at migration requirements and integrations. This provided a solid basis for selecting an enterprise experience platform.

​

The vendor demos were also built around real business requirements. Using actual content and specific country and brand scenarios made it much easier to see how well each platform would support the use cases that mattered in practice. The first rollout was deliberately designed as a scalable foundation. This meant the platform could be expanded step by step after go-live without creating unnecessary technical debt from the start.

Key Criteria for Choosing a CMS

Many CMS evaluations start with long feature lists and vendor comparisons. But a good selection process starts earlier. First, define your business goals and concrete use cases. Then involve the people who will actually work with the platform – from marketing and editorial teams to IT, security and operations. These requirements can then be turned into clear criteria for comparing different platforms.

Why CMS Architecture Matters

Features and usability are important, but so is the architecture behind the CMS. It determines how easily the platform can connect to your existing systems, how content can be delivered across different touchpoints and how well the setup can evolve over time.

​

Traditional CMS platforms typically combine content management and the frontend in one system. Modern platforms increasingly take a more flexible approach.

​

For companies with more complex digital requirements, two approaches are particularly relevant:

Integrated or DXP-Oriented Enterprise Platforms

  • Bring content management, governance and workflows together in one central platform
  • Often include built-in capabilities for personalization, asset management and marketing
  • Make it easier to manage content, processes and the digital experience centrally
  • Are a good fit for complex organizations with strong governance and scalability requirements

Composable or API-First Platforms

  • Use a modular architecture and structured content
  • Separate content management from content delivery and support headless setups
  • Can be connected flexibly to existing systems and solutions such as commerce, PIM, DAM or marketing platforms
  • Offer more flexibility, but can also require more effort when it comes to architecture, integration and operations

There is no universally right approach – and the most fashionable architecture is not necessarily the best one for your business.

​

What matters is what fits your existing systems, processes and long-term goals. Hybrid approaches are also possible, and in practice the boundaries between the different models are becoming increasingly blurred.

Learnings From the Field: Start Small, Then Scale Internationally

Our customer dmTECH was also looking for a new CMS to modernize the career platform for the entire dm Group. The project started with a pilot: the dmTECH career portal was rebuilt on the new CMS platform. The goal was to create an employer experience that felt more like the customer experience people already knew from dm’s retail business.

​

The team chose a composable CMS architecture, which also made it easier to connect additional systems. After the successful go-live, the platform was gradually rolled out to other dm country organizations. Today, the CMS is used in more than 14 countries. It makes content easier to manage, supports faster rollouts and continues to evolve.

​

The project shows why it pays to think about scalability from the very beginning. A clearly defined pilot can help you test the architecture and processes in a real-world setting before rolling them out to additional markets.

How Future-Proof Is Your New CMS?

Functionality and technical fit are important, but so is the future of the platform itself.

Choosing a CMS is usually a long-term decision. You want a platform that can keep up with your business, adapt to changing requirements and grow with you over the years. That’s why it’s worth taking a close look at the vendor and its wider ecosystem. Some useful indicators include:

  • A clear and transparent product roadmap
  • Ongoing cloud-native development
  • Well-documented APIs with long-term support
  • Regular security and platform updates
  • A strong partner and developer ecosystem
  • Transparent support and operating models
  • References from companies with similar size and complexity
  • An ecosystem that can also cover related needs such as DAM, analytics, testing, personalization or commerce

​

The key question is simple: Can the platform evolve with you?

​

For a technology decision that is likely to stay in place for years, don’t just look at what the platform can do today. Consider how both the platform and the vendor are likely to develop in the future.

​

​

Tip: Think About AI-Driven Content Processes Early

AI is becoming an increasingly important part of content management, so it should also be part of your evaluation. Over the next three to five years, AI-supported content and asset workflows, automated content supply chains, and faster creation and optimization of landing pages are likely to become standard.

​

AI-powered personalization at scale, smarter content modularization and automated content delivery across headless and hybrid setups will also become more important. The result is a shift away from simply managing content toward more connected, automated and data-driven content operations – with AI increasingly connecting content, commerce and data.

What Does a CMS Really Cost?

When planning a CMS budget, it’s easy to focus on license fees. But the total cost goes well beyond the license.

​

You should also consider the effort and cost involved in:

  • Strategy and conceptual work
  • Websites, countries, brands and languages
  • Migrating existing content and assets
  • Integrating other systems
  • Quality assurance and testing
  • Training and change management
  • Translation and data cleansing
  • Ongoing operations and development

​

It’s also worth looking beyond the direct cost of the platform when measuring ROI. A successful CMS can, for example, help shorten time to market, reduce manual work, make better use of existing content and speed up the rollout of new websites, brands and countries.

Choose Your CMS in 3 Steps

  1. Define Your Goals, Processes and Requirements: Before comparing platforms, make sure you know what you want the new CMS to achieve. Which business goals should it support? Which processes need to improve? And what will you need from the platform in the years ahead? This gives you a clear foundation for the technology decision.
  2. Compare Platforms Using Real Use Cases: Feature lists rarely tell the whole story. Real use cases and measurable business goals make it much easier to compare vendors in a meaningful way and see how their platforms perform in situations that actually matter to your teams.
  3. Get the Right Teams Involved Early: A CMS affects many parts of the business. Marketing, editorial, IT, data and security teams should therefore be involved from the beginning.

​

Bringing these perspectives together early helps you identify requirements, avoid surprises later and make a decision that works for the organization as a whole.

Conclusion

Choosing a new CMS is about more than picking the platform with the longest feature list. It’s about finding a solution that fits your business, your processes and your existing digital setup – and that can grow with you.

​

Our projects show how valuable it is to start with a clear target state, define real use cases and involve the right teams early on. Architecture, integration, scalability and the long-term direction of the platform and vendor all matter just as much as the features you need today. The right CMS is the one that supports your digital experience today while giving you enough flexibility to meet whatever comes next.
